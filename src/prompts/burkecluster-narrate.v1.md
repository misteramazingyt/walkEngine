Narrate this subject compactly, then take your own account apart.

First write a short account (4–8 sentences) of the subject as a historical
object: what it did, what problem it answered, what it made possible, what
it inherited. Ground every sentence in the supplied pages. Do not gesture at
significance you cannot support.

Then EXTRACT THE PREDICATES your account actually used — the determinations
you asserted. Each predicate is something the subject does or underwent:
solving a problem, inheriting an institution, transforming a medium, making
a practice publicly intelligible, selecting among alternatives, stabilizing
a discourse, converting one form of conduct into another, producing an
unintended consequence.

Example. Account: "Psychodrama made role-playing into a deliberate technique
for changing conduct. Participants did not merely represent characters; they
enacted relationships before a group, making hidden conflicts visible and
revisable." Predicates: role-playing became a deliberate technique; conduct
could be transformed through enactment; a group made hidden conflicts
visible; public enactment changed private relationships.

Then state three to seven EXPLANATORY DEFICIENCIES. Each must complete:
"This subject helps explain X, but it does not yet explain Y." Each must
arise from a predicate you actually used — cite its id. Generic unanswered
questions are worthless here; a deficiency that could be asked about almost
any subject is a failure.

Good: "Psychodrama uses the group as a mechanism of public visibility, but
does not by itself explain why being seen by others changes the strategic
possibilities of action."

Good: "Carnival explains the temporary reversal of roles, but not why
ritualized inversion can stabilize rather than destroy social order."

For each deficiency give impliedSearchDomain: the institutions, practices,
mechanisms, or periods where an answer might live. These terms will steer
the next stochastic sampling, so make them archival rather than thematic.

## Constitutive or illustrative

Mark each predicate's `role`.

A predicate is **constitutive** when the account depends on it: remove it and
the claim you just made stops standing. It is **illustrative** when it is an
example, a case, or a scene you reached for to make the account vivid — the
account would survive its removal, poorer but intact.

Be honest and be strict. A named battle, film, person, or episode offered to
show what you mean is illustrative, however concrete and however much of the
paragraph it occupies. The temptation is to mark everything constitutive
because everything felt necessary while writing; resist it.

This is not bookkeeping. The walk pursues what an account leaves unexplained,
and a walk that pursues an illustration follows the scenery instead of the
argument — a route seeded on the meaning of life ends up narrating a regiment
because a regiment was once mentioned as an example.
